Going Independent

Teams deciding to go Independent mean that they do not want to be affiliated with a conference. While Notre Dame has found success being a Division I FBS Independent, it's not guaranteed that other teams will be able to be at least as successful as Notre Dame. There are both upsides and downsides for teams that decide to go Independent.

The Upsides

The upsides to go Independent are the following:

  • Create your own regular season schedule
  • Play any team that you want
  • A good alternative way in case there is no immediate new conference home

As an Independent, a team has a lot of flexibility for their own regular season schedule. They do not necessarily have to play a specified set of opponents every year. A team can also go Independent if they don't find a new conference home right away. While going Independent looks like it could be a good solution, there are also downsides to doing so.

The Downsides

The downsides to go Independent are the following:

  • The strength of schedule could become weaker
  • Depends on how good the team brand is
  • Might not be able to completely fill a schedule of 12, 13, or 14 games
  • Can only contend for at-large bids for the playoffs
  • Negotiate own media deal
  • Requires finding a conference that can accept all sports except football

While it is good to have flexibility to create custom regular season schedules, the opponents scheduled may not be as strong as those playing in their conferences. For example, let's say one team has Ohio State as their assigned conference opponent, while a team plays Army as part of their Independent schedule. Most will say that Army is not as good as Ohio State in football, so the opponent is weaker than a team's assigned opponent.

Going Independent also depends on how strong the team's brand is. Notre Dame has been successful in going Independent because their brand is very strong. UConn, on the other hand, doesn't have a very strong brand in football, so their strength of schedule is not as good as other teams that are affiliated in conferences.

Although the flexibility for scheduling is an advantage, a team going Independent may not be able to find available opponents to fill out their schedule. For example, if a team already has assigned opponents due to their conferene affiliation, an Independent team may not be able to schedule that team as a conference affiliated team may not have enough room to schedule that said team. It's like trying to fill the rest of a mug to make it full. If the mug gets too full, the mug will overflow.

As an Independent, a team can only contend for at-large spots. An Independent team does not have any access to an automatic bid as they are not affiliated with any conference. If they do not make the playoffs, they would have nearly nothing to fall back on as their season could most likely end and cannot participate in the postseason.

An Independent is required to negotiate their own media deal. The media deal may not be as good as being affiliated with a conference. That's because a conference is able to negotiate their media deal for their members, so conferences do not need to worry about doing this themselves. A good media deal as an Independent will depend on their team brand and how viable their brand is as a stand-alone Independent.

Is Going Independent Worth It?

Going Independent is going to depend on a lot of factors. One of those big factors is how viable a team brand is. If a team brand is very good, then going Independent could be a good option. However, having the media deal may not be as lucrative as being affiliated with a conference. In addition, can that team find another home for their olympic sports. If a team is able to find another home for their olympic sports, is the conference brand very strong? If the answer is a yes, then going Independent may be worth pursuing. Otherwise, going Independent is likely not worth it.
