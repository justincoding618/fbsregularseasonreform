16-Team Conference

A conference that consists of 16 teams is where conference scheduling gets a bit difficult, but is ok to manage. The Big 12 and the SEC currently have 16 members. The SEC stayed with an 8-game conference schedule in 2024 and 2025 until moving up to a 9-game conference schedule. The Big 12 has continued to administer a 9-game conference schedule after expanding to 16 members. While a 9-game conference schedule would still work here, the possibility of going up to a 10 or an 11-game conference schedule should not be out of the possibility.

Possible Conference Scheduling

For a 16-team conference, the possible conference scheduling could look like this.

Flexible Scheduling

The Big 12 made a decision to continue administering a 9-game conference schedule. There, they only kept four games to be played annually. The protected games were the Holy War (BYU-Utah), Duel in the Desert (Arizona-Arizona State), Bluebonnet Battle (Baylor-TCU), and the Sunflower Showdown (Kansas-Kansas State). The rest of the Big 12 members played all the other teams within a 2-year period. This is the most flexible schedule as protecting only four games means that you can be creative with how conference schedules can be made. If a team has zero protected opponents, that means that all nine conference games are rotating opponents. This scheduling flexibility can also apply to any number of conference games (including 10 or 11-game conference schedules).

3+6 Schedule

In 2025, the SEC announced a 9-game conference schedule where each team had three protected opponents while rotating the other opponents. In other words, three out of the nine conference games were teams to play every season, while the six remaining games were rotating opponents. Teams would play the other opponents every other year. For a 16-team conference, this is one of the best ways to play all conference opponents within a reasonable time frame. Every team would be able to play all their opponents within a 2-year period. For a home-and-home of every conference opponent, that can be completed within a 4-year period. This scheduling only works for 9 conference games. If moving to more than 9 conference games, flexibile scheduling would be strongly recommended.

Pod Scheduling

Another option to manage conference schedules is to split teams into pods. For example, in a 16-team conference, teams would be split into four pods of four teams. The conference would rotate pods to play all teams in their pod, all teams in one pod, and then one team from all the other pods based on similar finish of each pod.

Let's do an example of the current Big 12 Conference. Let's say the pods are split as the following:

West Pod Central Pod Texas Pod East Pod
Arizona Colorado Baylor Cincinnati
Arizona State Kansas Houston Iowa State
BYU Kansas State TCU UCF
Utah Oklahoma State Texas Tech West Virginia

The conference schedule for the Big 12 would look something like this:

Pod Year A Year B Year C
West Pod All vs West Pod, All vs Central Pod, 1 from East Pod, 1 From Texas Pod All vs West Pod, All vs Texas Pod, 1 from East Pod, 1 From Central Pod All vs West Pod, All vs East Pod, 1 from Central Pod, 1 From Texas Pod
Central Pod All vs Central Pod, All vs West Pod, 1 from East Pod, 1 From Texas Pod All vs Central Pod, All vs East Pod, 1 from Texas Pod, 1 From West Pod All vs Central Pod, All vs Texas Pod, 1 from West Pod, 1 From East Pod
Texas Pod All vs Texas Pod, All vs East Pod, 1 from West Pod, 1 From Central Pod All vs Texas Pod, All vs West Pod, 1 from Central Pod, 1 From East Pod All vs Texas Pod, All vs Central Pod, 1 from East Pod, 1 From West Pod
East Pod All vs East Pod, All vs Central Pod, 1 from Texas Pod, 1 From West Pod All vs East Pod, All vs Texas Pod, 1 from West Pod, 1 From Central Pod All vs East Pod, All vs West Pod, 1 from Central Pod, 1 From Texas Pod

The one opponent from the two other pods would be based on similar finishes of the corresponding position of their own pod. For example, a team that finishes first in their pod would play teams that also finish 1st place in their respective pods when not playing other pods in a given year. Let's demonstrate. Let's say the most recent completed season had the pod standings that look like this (records are conference records):

West Pod Central Pod Texas Pod East Pod
Utah (9-0) Oklahoma State (6-3) Texas Tech (8-1) Iowa State (5-4)
BYU (7-2) Kansas (2-7) Houston (6-3) UCF (4-5)
Arizona (6-3) Colorado (2-7) Baylor (4-5) Cincinnati (3-6)
Arizona State (5-4) Kansas State (1-8) TCU (3-6) West Virginia (2-7)

Let's say the West Pod is playing all of the East Pod for the upcoming season. Utah's conference schedule would look something like this:

Conference Game Opponent
1 vs Iowa State
2 at Arizona State
3 vs Cincinnati
4 vs Oklahoma State
5 at Texas Tech
6 at West Virginia
7 vs Arizona
8 vs UCF
9 at BYU

As ssen here, Utah's two other conference opponents are based on their similar finish to their respective pods. For Utah, since they would finish 1st in their pod, Texas Tech (who would finish first in the Texas Pod) and Oklahoma State (who would finish first in the Central Pod) would be their other two conference opponents. While not a perfect solution, a 16-team conference administering a 9-game conference schedule based on pods would work. However, if administering more than 9 conference games, then pod scheduling would not work well. You would instead return to the flexibile scheduling scenario.

Even with pod scheduling, you can still have a standing of all the teams based on the conference record. The top team that has the best conference record would be named the conference champion. However, if two or more teams are tied for first, then tie-breakers would be applied.
